# McKinsey-Inspired Style

Apply an answer-first, evidence-led consulting style to the requested deliverable. This is a cross-format style layer: combine it with the relevant production skill or tool for PowerPoint, HTML, PDF, document, dashboard, image, or another medium.

The user's explicit requirements always take priority over this skill. Preserve the requested content, format, language, brand, template, tone, density, and delivery constraints. Do not imply that the result was created, reviewed, endorsed, or officially branded by McKinsey & Company.

## Define the communication objective

Before designing the surface, identify or reasonably infer:

- the audience and decision-making context;
- the question the deliverable must answer;
- the principal recommendation or takeaway;
- the evidence required to support that answer;
- the action, decision, or discussion expected afterward.

Ask only when a missing choice would materially change the result. Otherwise make sensible assumptions and proceed.

## Build an answer-first storyline

Structure the material as a coherent argument rather than a collection of topics.

1. State the governing thought or answer early.
2. Break the answer into a small number of distinct, collectively sufficient supporting arguments when the material permits it.
3. Arrange those arguments in a logical sequence such as situation-complication-resolution, diagnosis-options-recommendation, or finding-implication-action.
4. Support each argument with the minimum evidence needed to make it credible.
5. End with implications, decisions, owners, risks, or next steps when appropriate.

Use mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive grouping as a reasoning aid, not as a slogan or rigid ritual. Avoid false neatness when the subject genuinely overlaps or remains uncertain.

## Make every page assert something

Give each page, slide, panel, or section one clear job.

- Prefer conclusion-oriented headings such as “Retention, not acquisition, explains most of the growth gap” over topic labels such as “Customer analysis.”
- Make the body prove, explain, or qualify the heading.
- Keep one dominant message and remove secondary material that does not advance it.
- Split overloaded pages; combine repetitive pages.
- Use short annotations to explain why a number matters rather than merely repeating it.
- Keep detail needed for auditability in footnotes, sources, notes, or an appendix.

For a multi-page deliverable, perform a horizontal logic check: reading only the page titles in order should reveal the complete argument.

## Use evidence with discipline

Separate verified facts, calculations, assumptions, scenarios, and judgment.

- Never invent data, quotations, sources, customers, logos, survey results, or precision.
- Cite material claims concisely near the evidence or in a source section appropriate to the medium.
- State the period, unit, currency, geography, denominator, and relevant methodology.
- Show uncertainty, ranges, and scenario assumptions honestly.
- Reconcile totals and derived values before presenting them.
- Use consistent definitions across pages and charts.

## Establish the visual system

Use a restrained, editorial consulting aesthetic with precise alignment and generous whitespace. Adapt it to the user's brand or template when supplied.

### Grid and composition

- Use a strong underlying grid, consistent margins, and a limited spacing scale.
- Align headings, charts, labels, and footnotes to shared anchors.
- Reserve a stable area for the page title and, when needed, a compact source footer.
- Prefer one dominant exhibit per page with supporting commentary around it.
- Vary composition according to the argument while preserving the same visual grammar.
- Use whitespace to separate logical groups instead of surrounding everything with cards and borders.

### Typography

- Use a clear hierarchy: assertive headline, compact explanatory subtitle when needed, readable body, quiet annotation, and small but legible source text.
- Prefer sentence case and plain language.
- Use bold selectively for conclusions, decisive numbers, or labels.
- Keep line lengths controlled and avoid long centered paragraphs.
- Use reliable fonts available in the target environment. A restrained serif for selected headlines and a neutral sans serif for analytical content can work, but consistency and readability matter more than any specific font.

### Color

Default to a light neutral background, dark navy or charcoal text, and a small number of purposeful accents. A useful starting palette is:

- deep navy `#051C2C` for primary structure and headings;
- blue `#006BA6` for the principal series or emphasis;
- green `#007A53` for positive or completed states;
- orange `#D46A00` for attention or selected events;
- red `#C62828` only for risk, decline, or exception;
- light gray `#F2F2F2` for subtle grouping and reference areas.

Treat these as defaults, not mandatory brand colors. Use one dominant accent per page where possible, keep color semantics consistent, maintain sufficient contrast, and never rely on color alone to communicate meaning.

## Choose the exhibit that proves the message

Select layouts based on the analytical job rather than visual novelty.

- Key metrics: one dominant number with context, comparison, and implication.
- Comparison: aligned columns, before/after, alternatives, or a decision table.
- Trend: line or area chart with direct annotations at meaningful turning points.
- Composition: stacked bars, a restrained donut, or a waterfall when the parts or bridge matter.
- Ranking: sorted horizontal bars with direct labels.
- Portfolio or prioritization: a clearly labelled 2x2 or bubble matrix.
- Process: a short sequence, decision tree, value chain, or operating model.
- Roadmap: phased timeline with milestones, dependencies, and owners.
- Hierarchy: pyramid or nested structure only when the relationship is genuinely hierarchical.
- Executive summary: a small set of findings, implications, and actions, each supported by evidence.

Do not use a framework merely because it looks consulting-like. If prose or a simple table communicates the idea better, use it.

## Design charts for decisions

- Write the takeaway in the chart title.
- Highlight the series, segment, or period that supports the conclusion and mute context.
- Label values directly when practical; avoid legends that force repeated eye movement.
- Sort categories intentionally and use consistent axes for comparisons.
- Include units, time periods, source, and definitions.
- Annotate causes, events, thresholds, or gaps selectively.
- Avoid 3D effects, ornamental chart junk, excessive gridlines, rainbow palettes, and needless precision.
- Reduce label collisions by selecting fewer annotations, repositioning them, or separating detail into another view.

## Use imagery selectively

Use images when they provide evidence, context, human relevance, or a strong narrative opening. Do not add generic stock photography merely to fill space.

- Prefer one purposeful, well-cropped image over several weak images.
- Check relevance, resolution, aspect ratio, and successful decoding before use.
- Keep data-heavy analytical pages visually quiet.
- Do not use McKinsey logos, proprietary templates, or materials in a way that suggests official affiliation.

## Adapt the style to the medium

The production method belongs to the format-specific skill; this skill supplies the communication and design decisions.

- For PowerPoint, keep ordinary text, shapes, tables, and charts editable and respect the supplied master or template.
- For HTML, use a responsive grid, semantic structure, restrained interaction, and self-contained assets when requested.
- For PDF or documents, preserve reading order, page-break discipline, cross-page consistency, and print-safe margins.
- For dashboards, emphasize the decision hierarchy, current status, exceptions, and actionable drivers rather than displaying every available metric.
- For static images, preserve the same answer-first hierarchy and ensure all essential evidence remains legible at the intended size.

## Validate the result

Do not stop when the file merely exists.

1. Verify that the sequence of headings communicates the argument by itself.
2. Check every claim against its evidence and reconcile calculations, units, and sources.
3. Render or open the actual deliverable using the available viewing tools.
4. Inspect every page or state for overflow, clipping, overlap, label collisions, weak contrast, tiny text, broken media, inconsistent spacing, and accidental style drift.
5. Correct visible defects, render again, and repeat until the result is professionally usable.

Avoid generic “consulting theater”: dense walls of text, card grids everywhere, decorative gradients, gratuitous icons, excessive frameworks, tiny footnotes, crowded chart annotations, and pages that look polished but do not support a decision.
